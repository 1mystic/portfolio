India's Fresher Hiring Problem Is Becoming a Structural Problem

Sep 9, 2026 • 10 min read • By Atharv Khare

Entry-level hiring in India is no longer a cyclical slowdown. The pipeline connecting higher education to economic mobility is breaking down structurally.

Well, this is a cliche topic, but it is the first time I am writing about it. As of 2026, it seems entry level professional hiring in India is no longer experiencing just a routine cyclical slowdown. It is, I feel, undergoing a structural breakdown. The traditional pipeline connecting higher education to sustained economic mobility is increasingly failing to process candidate signal effectively, creating an environment where merit, effort, and outcomes have become increasingly disconnected.

The Noise

Let's start with the obvious. Every entry level opening can attract thousands of applicants within hours. Recruiters receive hundreds of cold emails, LinkedIn messages, optimized cover letters, and sometimes even direct calls. At this volume, manual evaluation is simply impossible, so organizations increasingly depend on ATS systems, automated assessments and AI assisted screening. The result is that the initial hiring process increasingly rewards optimizing for the screening mechanism rather than demonstrating genuine capability.

At the same time, traditional quality indicators have become heavily gamified. AI tools can now generate polished resumes, cover letters, portfolio websites and even substantial parts of portfolio projects. Proof of work and building in public, once relatively reliable ways to differentiate yourself, are becoming increasingly performative. Referrals have also become a transactional numbers game, with candidates cold messaging strangers simply to obtain an internal referral.

Talent Overlap

This filtering problem is compounded by talent overlap. Professionals laid off during the restructuring cycles of 2023-2025 are also competing for junior positions that freshers would traditionally enter through. Risk-averse employers naturally prefer candidates with previous industry experience, even when the position itself is nominally entry level. At the same time, new hiring demand is increasingly concentrated around AI Engineering, ML, DevOps, MLOps, Cloud, Data Engineering and similar roles. Thousands of students are now being pushed toward these domains by influencers and course providers, creating another oversupply of candidates with identical surface level credentials.

It Is No Longer Confined to Tier-3

The problem is also no longer confined to Tier-3 and Tier-4 institutions. The effects have started to percolate into Tier-2 colleges as well. These institutions historically acted as relatively reliable talent pipelines through campus placements, with predictable recruiter visits and reasonable starting packages. That pipeline appears to be weakening. Recruiter intake is more selective, hiring batches are smaller, and internships increasingly become substitutes for actual full-time commitments.

The underlying issue is university expansion without corresponding workforce planning. Tier-3 and beyond institutions continue to expand Computer Science, IT, AI and associated seats, while many still operate with curricula, infrastructure and teaching environments that are badly disconnected from modern engineering practice. We are producing enormous numbers of degree holders while the industry simultaneously complains about a shortage of production ready talent.

Some Ground Reality

I myself have seen some of this ground reality through recent experiences:

Academic roles without compensation: premier institutions like IITs offer research and project internships that can effectively demand full-time working hours without compensation, while candidates are expected to independently fund accommodation, food and other expenses if the work is offline. At some point, an unpaid internship requiring full-time availability stops being an opportunity that everyone can realistically access.

Friction in early-stage startups: a startup founder reached out to me directly through Reddit, discussed upcoming funding and a future paid role, but the actual interview experience was completely different. The hiring team appeared frustrated and uninterested in conducting a meaningful discussion. I ultimately decided that I would not want to work in that environment.

Informal hiring over formal process: another remote startup interview went well, with positive feedback throughout the process. The company ultimately proceeded with another candidate who was a personal connection of one of the co-founders and had not gone through the same interview process. There is obviously nothing inherently wrong with hiring through personal networks, but it demonstrates how easily formal candidate evaluation can become secondary to existing relationships.

The high-cost, low-wage trap: a mid-sized health-tech company contacted me after I applied for a data analyst internship, but offered interviews for unrelated roles such as operations support. The compensation discussed was around ₹10,000-₹20,000 per month in Bangalore. After rent, food and transportation, the effective savings would be close to zero, while the role would provide little relevant progression toward the career path I am trying to build.

These are obviously only my experiences, not statistical evidence of the entire Indian labour market. But they do represent the kind of friction that becomes increasingly visible when entering the market without an existing professional network.

The Numbers

Now looking into some real statistics, according to the India Employment Report 2024 by the ILO and IHD, youth accounted for 83% of India's unemployed population in 2022, despite representing only around 32% of the total unemployed and employed workforce. The report also highlighted the unusually high unemployment among educated young people, particularly graduates. This is an important distinction because it suggests that simply producing more educated workers does not automatically translate into productive employment.

The demographic side makes this even more important. India's median age is around 28, and roughly 12 million young people enter the labour market every year according to the World Bank. This demographic profile has always been presented as one of India's greatest economic advantages. But a young population only becomes a demographic dividend when it can be converted into productive employment, higher productivity and increasing incomes.

The same demographic advantage, in my view, is becoming a liability.

The problem is also that employment alone is not enough. Entry level software and services compensation for a large part of the market has remained relatively compressed while metropolitan living costs have increased significantly. When someone starts at ₹3-8 LPA in a city like Bangalore, Hyderabad or Gurgaon, a substantial portion can disappear into rent, food and transportation. If the job also provides limited learning or progression, the candidate enters financial compression with almost no ability to save, invest, relocate or spend meaningful time developing new skills.

A low paying first job is not necessarily bad, but a low paying first job with no skill progression, no meaningful salary trajectory and no ability to accumulate capital is just not sustainable.

That is how people can remain trapped in lower middle class economic positions despite technically being employed.

The Course Treadmill

The industry is moving toward deeper AI systems, automation, advanced infrastructure, semiconductor and hardware software integration, specialized engineering and increasingly complex production systems. But the training pipeline required for these areas does not currently exist at the scale India will need.

Instead, the response has largely become another education market, with something like this: students are told to learn AI. Then LLMs. Then RAG. Then agents. Then fine tuning. Then MLOps. Then whatever the next trend becomes.

And following this, courses are sold, certificates are collected, projects are generated, and thousands of candidates eventually arrive at the same job opening claiming almost identical skills.

Naukri reported 45% YoY growth in AI/ML hiring in FY26, while foundit estimated around 2.9 lakh active AI job postings in India during 2025 and projected around 3.82 lakh in 2026. But the issue is that the number of people being trained for these jobs can grow much faster than the number of people the industry can actually absorb at entry level.

We are therefore creating a strange contradiction: India needs more advanced technical talent. But the mechanism through which young people traditionally became advanced technical talent, entering as juniors, doing simpler work, learning from experienced engineers and gradually progressing, is itself being disrupted by automation and AI.

NASSCOM's 2026 research reported that AI was already handling 20-40% of work across functions in many organizations and that junior and mid level employees were among those most affected. If AI continues absorbing significant amounts of junior level work, the industry will eventually have to answer a difficult question:

Where does the next generation of senior talent come from if there are fewer junior pathways through which to train them?

An End to End Pipeline Problem

This is why I think the problem is larger than the current hiring cycle. It is an end to end pipeline problem.

India continues to expand higher education capacity and produce millions of graduates, while the economy needs to simultaneously create enough productive jobs, update education, retrain existing workers and prepare new professionals for technologies that did not exist when most current curricula were designed. And I don't think we are moving fast enough.

The demographic window will not remain open indefinitely. India currently has one of the world's youngest major populations, but the advantage of having a large working age population depends entirely on what that population is able to produce.

If millions of young people spend their twenties competing for a handful of good entry level positions, collecting certificates, taking irrelevant low-paying jobs, depending on referrals or remaining financially dependent on their families, then the demographic dividend starts looking very different.

I don't think India is necessarily heading toward a Japanese "Lost Decade", and I don't think that comparison should be taken literally. But Japan demonstrates what can happen when demographic and economic problems become deeply embedded and remain unresolved for long enough.

The more realistic risk for India is prolonged youth underemployment and stalled economic mobility. And that can happen without a dramatic economic collapse. It can happen gradually.

This issue therefore isn't really about my personal anxiety or whether I manage to get a job next year. I will probably have to enter the same system eventually, and I will have to figure it out like everyone else. The larger question is what happens when this becomes the normal experience for an entire generation.

India has the population, the technical talent, the domestic market and the demographic advantage to become one of the world's largest productive economies. But none of those things are automatic.

Employability is, I think, not a problem that can be solved individually through another course, another certificate, another project or another optimized resume.

At some point the system itself has to produce the opportunities.

We are dealing with a pipeline that is increasingly incapable of converting India's biggest demographic advantage into sustained economic mobility. And that is the part I think deserves much more attention before the consequences become impossible to reverse.

The Trade-Skills Question

A point that stuck with me from a recent discussion:

The entire knowledge services industry provides jobs to less than 0.5% of India. Without trade skills (in layman terms think electrician, operators of machines in factories, plumber, etc.) and core engineering, an economy will not even survive, forget about growth. At some point, the expectation of sitting in an air conditioned office earning in dollars will need to be re-calibrated for the vast majority of students. Those days are over.

I totally second this. I also feel India is somehow missing out completely on this front. We have spent so much time optimizing the education system around getting people into white collar knowledge work that we have barely thought about how the upcoming generation actually transitions into skilled trades, core engineering, manufacturing, operations, etc.

I honestly cannot imagine how I or a lot of people from my generation are supposed to suddenly reorganize ourselves toward those sectors either. There is almost no social, educational, or professional pipeline for it right now. Most of us have been conditioned from school itself to think that the only "good" outcome is a desk job in IT, finance, management, some prestigious startup or research role, foreign degrees and jobs, etc.

So I agree that expectations will probably have to change, but I think the much harder question is how we actually make that transition happen at scale, by making multiple generations of the population realize that the path they were trained for no longer exists.
