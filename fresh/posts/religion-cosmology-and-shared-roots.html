Religion, Cosmology and the Shared Roots of Belief

Oct 25, 2026 • 25 min read • By Atharv Khare

How did the universe begin, and what do religions across the world say about it?

In this post, I want to look at some of the questions that religions across the world have tried to answer. How did the universe begin? Is there a power that created it, one that keeps it running, or a cycle in which everything eventually ends and begins again? Why do people suffer, what makes an action right or wrong, and what happens after death? Different religions give very different answers, but it is interesting to see where their stories resemble each other and where they diverge.

I will start with a few examples from the wider world, then move towards India and the many traditions now grouped under Hinduism. This is not an attempt to prove that one religion is right or that all religions are the same. It is an effort to understand the stories, their history, and what people have found meaningful in them. Readers from Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Jewish, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Zoroastrian, Indigenous, and other traditions are very welcome to add their own stories, corrections, and sources, and so are people who are not religious. The aim is to learn from one another, not to convert anyone or turn this into a competition.

A note on the word mythology: in the study of religion, a myth is a sacred or culturally important story. It doesn't mean the story is false or that believers are foolish. We can study a religious story respectfully while being clear about what history and archaeology can, and cannot, establish.

The Origins of Civilizations and Shared Religious Roots

The earliest human civilizations flourished in four major hotspots: Mesopotamia, Ancient Egypt, Ancient India, and Ancient China. Mesopotamia and Egypt eventually lost their original religions and cultural continuity through foreign conquests, while India and China survived as the world's two continuous ancient civilizations. Population concentrated and grew in these two, which maintained a rich culture with extreme diversity and continuous adaptation over thousands of years.

For most of recorded history, India and China produced the vast majority of the world's goods and services. Being resource-rich and economically developed, their rulers never felt the need to launch global imperial marches. Warfare was mostly limited to internal rivalries among local rulers.

India did not conquer foreign lands, but it served as the intellectual exporter of the ancient world. It exported early ideas of spiritualism, astronomy, astrology, mathematics (including zero and the decimal system), architecture, civil engineering, chemistry, metallurgy, geography, and geology. These ideas reached Egypt and Mesopotamia through maritime and overland trade. Because India stayed in constant touch with the Middle East, Persian and Arab empires carried these concepts into Europe, where they were developed into local Western forms and popularized globally through European empires.

This ancient network of trade and prehistoric migration also helps explain the similarities between religions. Within India, Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism share clear traits because they come from the same parent tradition. We also see striking parallels across Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and ancient European mythologies. These ideas developed in the India-China and Middle Eastern corridors, were passed down orally for generations, and slowly diverged as people migrated and built independent religions.

Recurring godly characters appear across cultures. The Vedic Indra, the Greek Zeus, and the Norse Thor are all sky and storm deities who wield lightning bolts and battle giant serpents. A sacred trinity appears in almost every major tradition, from the Hindu Trimurti to Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Christian trinities. Even specific narratives repeat. Noah's Ark in the Abrahamic religions mirrors the Mesopotamian flood story of Gilgamesh and the Hindu story of Manu and the horned fish, showing how ancient memories branched into local forms while teaching the same core ethical practices.

Deep Antiquity and the Hindu Scriptures

Tracing Hindu ideas through prehistoric memory and archaeology takes us back to around 30,000 BCE, and these traditions may be even older. Rock paintings in central India (such as Bhimbetka) date back 30,000 years, and stone goddess shrines (like the Baghor stone yantra) date to around 10,000 BCE, followed later by bronze idols and artifacts of the Indus-Saraswati era. In those early days, these were independent local ideas and ways of life spread across the subcontinent. Around 12,000 to 10,000 BCE, as the Ice Age ended and river settlements grew, these traditions began converging into what we now recognize as Hinduism.

For thousands of years, sacred teachings were passed down orally before being written. In traditional Indian timelines, the earliest codified records are the Vedas, composed in ancient Vedic Sanskrit (alongside later literature in Classical Sanskrit and Prakrit) around 5000 to 3000 BCE, though Western academics give later dates. After the four Vedas came the Brahmanas, the philosophical Upanishads, the great epics, and the Puranas. Each covers different gods and describes varying creation myths based on its philosophical approach.

The Multiverse and the Origin of the Universe

Of the many creation myths in Hindu scriptures, the Puranic cosmic account makes the most logical sense to me.

Beyond space, time, and atomic matter lies the transcendental plane (Paravyoma, the higher-dimensional reality), governed by three foundational principles above human comprehension:

  • Karanodakashayi Vishnu (Mahavishnu): The supreme macro-cosmic consciousness reclining on the Causal Ocean (Karana Ocean). He doesn't build universes with hands; he dreams them. As he exhales, countless golden cosmic spheres (Brahmandas, or universes) emerge from the pores of his skin. When he inhales after billions of years, they contract and are drawn back into his body, dissolving into potentiality.
  • Shiva (the Infinite Pillar of Nothingness / Shunya): If Mahavishnu is the dreamer, the infinite void and substratum in which universes manifest and dissolve is Shiva (from Shi-va, "that which is not," or pure unmanifest consciousness). He is the pillar of stillness, the cosmic hard-drive and dimensional canvas holding the multiverse together.
  • Adi Parashakti (the Primordial Cosmic Energy): Pure consciousness cannot manifest into physical reality without kinetic energy. Shakti is the dynamic cosmic energy (Prakriti / Maya) that powers every breath of expansion, binding consciousness into matter, force, and life. Without Shakti, Shiva is inert; without Shiva, Shakti has no container.

Within a single cosmic egg (Brahmanda), our universe, the macro consciousness projects a localized avatar known as Garbhodakashayi Vishnu. In the primordial darkness, floating on Brahman (the cosmic ocean of undifferentiated existence) and resting on the coils of the serpent Ananta Shesha (timeless awareness), Vishnu lies in yogic sleep (Yoga-Nidra).

Brahma, Madhu and Kaitabha, and the Birth of the Earth

From Vishnu's navel sprouts a luminescent lotus stem. When it blooms, Brahma is born in its petals, alone, disoriented, and without memory. He climbs down the stem to find his origin, fails to reach the end, and returns to his lotus seat.

As Vishnu sleeps, two drops of wax (Mala) slip from his ears. Reacting with the primal waters and Tamas (inertia/chaos), they solidify into two ferocious entities: Madhu and Kaitabha. Seeing the vulnerable Brahma, they charge up the stem to devour him. Terrified, Brahma invokes Yoga-Maya (the sleep-energy of Shakti resting on Vishnu's eyes), begging her to wake Vishnu.

Vishnu awakens, eyes shining like twin suns, and wrestles the demons for five thousand celestial years. He realizes they have a boon: they can die only when they consent. Using divine wit, Vishnu praises their strength and offers them a boon. Blinded by arrogance, they laugh and tell him to ask them instead. Vishnu immediately asks for their deaths. Seeing only endless ocean, they stipulate: "Slay us only in a place that is completely dry and uncovered by water." Vishnu expands his cosmic form, lifts them onto his colossal, dry thighs above the waters, and severs their heads with his discus.

Their fat, marrow, and remains (Medini) spread across the waters and coagulate into the solid crust of the Earth (Prithvi / Medini). With chaos subdued, Vishnu transmits the four Vedas and the blueprint of cosmic order into Brahma's mind, making him the all-knowing architect of the material universe.

The Pillar of Fire and Brahma's Fifth Head

As Brahma engineers the cosmos, he has five heads: four facing the cardinal directions and one facing upward. Overwhelmed by his own intellect, the fifth head develops Ahamkara (ego). Brahma declares to Vishnu that as the active creator of all worlds, he alone deserves worship as the supreme deity.

As their argument escalates, space rips open between them. An infinite, blazing pillar of fire and nothingness (Jyotirlinga), the intra-universal manifestation of Shiva, erupts before them, with no top or bottom. A voice from the pillar says that whoever finds its crown or base will be acknowledged as supreme.

  • Vishnu's quest: As a wild boar (Varaha), Vishnu plunges downward for thousands of ages. Realizing the pillar is infinite, the boundless Absolute, he humbly accepts the truth, returns, and bows to the pillar in honest admission.
  • Brahma's deception: As a white swan (Hamsa), Brahma flies upward at the speed of thought. Exhausted after aeons, he sees a Ketaki flower (screwpine blossom) drifting down through the void. Instead of accepting his limit, his fifth head devises a lie. He persuades the flower to testify falsely that Brahma retrieved it from the top of the pillar.

When Brahma proclaims his fabricated victory, backed by the flower's testimony, the pillar splits open. Because the three supreme cosmic functions (Trimurti: Creation, Preservation, Dissolution) must remain pure and free from egoic corruption, Shiva steps forth as Bhairava, wielding his Trishula (trident). Condemning Brahma's arrogance and dishonesty, he slices off the fifth head. He also decrees that Vishnu, who spoke the truth, will be worshipped across all ages in grand temples on Earth, while Brahma will have almost no temples or formal cult among mortals, and the Ketaki flower will be forever barred from Shiva's rituals. Chastened, the four-headed Brahma resumes creation with humility.

The Trimurti and Their Consorts (The Tridevi)

Once the universe is structured, its operations are divided among the Trimurti: Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva. Each has a distinct cosmic role and a female consort (the Tridevi, manifestations of Shakti) who provides the power that role needs.

Brahma, the Creator. Creating a universe requires creativity, knowledge, language, music, and the arts, so his consort is Saraswati. She appeared from Brahma's meditative mind and vocal vibration, holding a Veena and scriptures, bringing the wisdom and artistic order that turn chaotic matter into a structured cosmos.

Vishnu, the Preserver. Maintaining a universe requires prosperity, wealth, resources, nourishment, and beauty, so his consort is Lakshmi. She appeared from the cosmic ocean, seated on a lotus, during the churning of the ocean and chose Vishnu as her eternal partner. Because we humans and all living creatures live inside the ongoing maintenance phase of the universe, we primarily worship Vishnu and his avatars, who is directly in charge of universal action and preservation.

Shiva, the Destroyer. He dissolves the universe at the end of its cycle so new creation can occur. Destruction and transformation require immense raw energy, so his consort is Shakti as Sati and later Parvati (who also takes fierce forms like Durga and Kali). She first appeared as the daughter of Daksha, then reincarnated as the daughter of the Himalayas, performing deep penance to unite with Shiva. While Vishnu presides over civilized society and heaven, Shiva also looks after the beings rejected by the normal world, including phantoms, ghosts, spirits, wild beasts, and wandering ascetics, showing that pure consciousness accepts everything without bias.

The Seven Sages, the 33 Categories of Gods, and Cosmic Weapons

To populate the universe, Brahma doesn't initially use biological reproduction. Through pure mental intention he manifests the Manasa-Putras (mind-born sons), the Saptarishis or Seven Great Sages (traditionally Marichi, Atri, Angirasa, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, and Vashistha, alongside figures like Bhrigu, Daksha, and Narada). Because they are created from cosmic mind rather than a womb, they transcend ordinary mortality, serving as immortal administrators and guardians of Vedic wisdom across the ages.

From Marichi's lineage comes Maharishi Kashyapa, who marries the daughters of Prajapati Daksha, most notably the sisters Aditi (undivided cosmic unity and light) and Diti (division and dualistic ambition), along with others like Danu and Kadru:

  • The Adityas (Devas / Gods): Born from Aditi and Kashyapa, they hold dominion over Swarga (Heaven) and the subtle realms. The phrase often mistranslated as "33 million gods" is actually Trayastrimshat Koti, where Koti means "types" or "categories": 33 supreme functional deities (12 Adityas, 11 Rudras, 8 Vasus, and 2 Ashvins), later expanded by poetic imagination into 330 million manifestations. Each Deva functions as a specific cosmic program or natural law.
  • The Daityas and Danavas (Asuras): Born from Diti and Danu, they are half-brothers of the Devas and hold dominion over Patala, the subterranean realms. Patala is often conflated with "hell" in Western terms, but Puranic texts describe it as a realm of architectural splendor, jewels, and sensory wealth. They represent material ambition, primal power, and egoic resistance to cosmic order.
  • Swayambhuva Manu and Shatarupa: To start human life, Brahma splits himself into male (Swayambhuva Manu, whose name gives us the Sanskrit Manava and English Man) and female (Shatarupa, "she of a hundred forms"). From their union descends the human race, tasked with exercising free will and Dharma on Earth.

Cosmic weapons. When the Sun god Surya burned too brightly for his wife Sanjna to bear, the divine architect Vishwakarma placed the Sun on a lathe and shaved off a portion of its energy. From that solar matter he forged Shiva's Trishula and Vishnu's Sudarshana Chakra. Indra's Vajra was forged from the spine of the self-sacrificing sage Dadhichi.

The Ten Avatars of Vishnu (Dashavatara)

Whenever the universe tips toward chaos (Adharma), Vishnu projects an Avatara (literally a "descent" of consciousness into matter) onto the earthly plane. The sequence of the Dashavatara strikingly mirrors both biological evolution (aquatic life, amphibians, land mammals, hominids, then human civilization) and the psychological evolution of human society.

  • Matsya (Fish): Mirrors the Noah's Ark story. As a horned fish, Vishnu warns King Manu of a great deluge and tows his ark, carrying the seven sages and the seeds of all life, to safety in the Himalayas.
  • Kurma (Turtle): A giant tortoise supporting Mount Mandara on his back during the churning of the cosmic ocean.
  • Varaha (Boar): Dives into the cosmic waters, defeats the demon Hiranyaksha, and lifts the Earth on his tusks.
  • Narasimha (Half-Man, Half-Lion): Hiranyakashipu could not be killed by man or beast, indoors or outdoors, day or night, or by any weapon. Vishnu bursts from a pillar at twilight and slays him with his claws, on his lap, at a threshold.
  • Vamana (Dwarf): The righteous Daitya king Mahabali conquered heaven. Vamana came to his sacrifice as a dwarf boy and asked for three steps of land. When Bali agreed, he grew to giant size and covered Earth and sky in two steps. For the third, Bali humbly offered his own head. Impressed, Vishnu granted him rule over the subterranean realm of Sutala and promised he would become the next Indra in the next cosmic cycle.
  • Parashurama (Violent Warrior-Sage): A fierce sage with Shiva's axe who wiped out corrupt warrior kings abusing their power.
  • Rama (Peaceful, Ideal Man): Hero of the Ramayana, embodying peace, moral duty, and honest kingship, who defeats the tyrant Ravana.
  • Krishna (Smart Administrator and Strategist): The statesman of the Mahabharata who uses sharp strategy to establish justice in a morally complex world and delivers the Bhagavad Gita.
  • Buddha (Spiritual Man): Adopted into the Puranic framework as the teacher who reformed society away from corrupt rituals toward non-violence and meditation.
  • Kalki (Final Warrior): Prophesied to arrive at the end of the current Kali Yuga to end ultimate corruption and restart the cycle.

Cosmic Time Cycles and Precise Ancient Calculations

One of the most fascinating aspects of ancient Indian thought is the scale on which it describes time. Hindu texts describe the universe moving through repeated cycles of creation, preservation, destruction, and renewal, from small units of time to periods of billions or even hundreds of trillions of years. These ideas show how Indian philosophers and astronomers approached questions about time, the cosmos, and humanity's place in it. At the same time, traditional cosmological calculations and scientific measurements answer different kinds of questions, so they should be compared carefully.

The Four Yugas

According to Hindu cosmology, time moves through a repeating cycle of four ages called a Mahayuga. Each represents a gradual change in the world's moral and spiritual condition, with truth and righteousness declining as the cycle progresses.

YugaTraditional duration (human years)Traditional description
Satya or Krita Yuga1,728,000Age of truth, virtue, and spiritual harmony

One Mahayuga4,320,000One complete cycle of the four ages

These durations follow the traditional ratio of 4:3:2:1. In the conventional calculation, a Mahayuga is 12,000 divine years, with one divine year equal to 360 human years.

By the traditional chronology, Kali Yuga began in 3102 BCE. By 2026 CE, about 5,127 years have elapsed, leaving the vast majority of its stated duration to come. This date belongs to the traditional Hindu system of time reckoning and should not be confused with a scientifically established date for the start of a historical era.

From Millions to Billions and Trillions of Years

A thousand Mahayugas make one daytime of Brahma, called a Kalpa. It is followed by an equally long night, during which the manifested world dissolves.

A Kalpa is conventionally organized into 14 Manvantaras plus transitional periods called Sandhyas, which together add up to 1,000 Mahayugas: 1,000 × 4,320,000 = 4,320,000,000, so Brahma's daytime lasts 4.32 billion human years.

This number is interesting because modern science puts the age of the Earth at about 4.54 billion years (USGS: The age of Earth). But numerical similarity doesn't show that the Kalpa was meant as a measure of Earth's geological age. In Hindu cosmology, it is a period within a much larger account of cosmic time.

A full lifespan of Brahma is 100 × 360 × 8.64 × 10⁹ = 311.04 × 10¹², or 311.04 trillion human years. In the traditions that use this reckoning, the end of Brahma's lifespan is associated with a more profound dissolution of the manifested cosmos.

Traditional Hindu ritual formulas place the present period in Brahma's 51st year, in the Shveta-Varaha Kalpa, the Vaivasvata Manvantara, and the 28th Mahayuga. These details are part of the Sankalpa, the ritual statement of time and place recited in many Hindu ceremonies. They represent a religious cosmological chronology, not an independently verified scientific timeline.

Astronomical Calculations in Ancient India

Alongside cosmological speculation, India developed substantial traditions of mathematical astronomy. Texts such as the Surya Siddhanta describe methods for calculating planetary motions, calendrical cycles, eclipses, and other phenomena. Astronomers worked with naked-eye observations, geometric models, mathematical tables, gnomons for tracking shadows, water clocks, and instruments representing the celestial sphere. Their methods developed over centuries, with scholars revising earlier calculations and producing new tables.

Results were not identical across texts, but several traditional values are close to modern measurements:

These examples show the mathematical sophistication of Indian astronomy, and also why each calculation should be evaluated separately. Some values are remarkably close; others differ measurably, and values vary between texts, recensions, and methods. The interesting question isn't whether every ancient number matches a modern one, but how astronomers built mathematical systems that could predict celestial events with the tools available to them.

The Speed of Light in Sayana's Commentary

One frequently discussed example concerns Sayana, a fourteenth-century scholar who wrote a commentary on the Rigveda. A passage attributed to an earlier tradition states that the Sun travels 2,202 yojanas in half a nimesha. Some modern interpretations convert these units into a speed close to the modern speed of light, which is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, or about 186,282 miles per second (NIST).

The interpretation remains debated. The length of a yojana and the duration of a nimesha varied across textual traditions, and the passage doesn't unambiguously show that it was meant as a measured physical constant. Some scholars argue the correspondence is significant; others find the modern interpretation insufficiently supported. It is more accurate to call this an intriguing historical and mathematical question than to claim medieval Indian scholars conclusively measured the speed of light (Subhash Kak, The Speed of Light and Puranic Cosmology).

The Earth-Sun Distance and the Hanuman Chalisa

A similar claim concerns the line from the Hanuman Chalisa: Yuga sahasra yojana para bhanu. It appears in the story of the young Hanuman leaping toward the Sun, which he mistakes for a fruit. Under one popular interpretation, yuga is taken as 12,000, sahasra as 1,000, and one yojana as roughly eight miles. Multiplying gives 96 million miles. The modern mean Earth-Sun distance is about 93 million miles (149.6 million km).

The figures look close, but the interpretation depends on choosing particular definitions for the words and units. The verse is a devotional description of Hanuman's extraordinary leap, not an explicit astronomical measurement, so it would be premature to treat the resemblance as proof that the poem encoded a measured distance. This doesn't diminish the verse's cultural or literary significance. It simply separates the original story from a modern mathematical reading.

Core Teachings and the Development of Religious Traditions

Beyond cosmology, Hindu philosophy explores conduct, responsibility, consciousness, suffering, and liberation. The concepts of dharma, karma, samsara, and moksha are especially important, though their precise meanings vary across schools.

  • Dharma: duty, right conduct, moral order, and the responsibilities of one's circumstances. It is not a universal list of rules; different traditions interpret it through ethical reasoning, social responsibilities, religious duties, and the order of the cosmos.
  • Karma: action and its consequences. In many traditions, actions shape future experiences and influence rebirth. It is not simply the idea that everything good or bad that happens to a person is a direct reward or punishment for a past action.
  • Samsara and Moksha: samsara is the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth; moksha is liberation from it. Schools explain liberation differently. Advaita Vedanta understands it as realizing the identity of Atman and Brahman, while other schools maintain important distinctions between the individual self and the Supreme Being.

Together, these ideas place human life within a larger framework of ethical responsibility, spiritual development, and the search for liberation.

Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, and the Evolution of Indian Traditions

Religious traditions don't develop in isolation. India's history shows communities debating, challenging, and influencing one another for centuries.

  • Buddhism emerged in ancient India through Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, in an environment that included Vedic traditions and many renunciant movements. He emphasized the causes of suffering, ethical conduct, meditation, and the path to liberation. Buddhist traditions challenged several ideas and practices associated with Brahmanical authority, but Buddhism is not simply a reformed branch of Hinduism. It developed its own doctrines, communities, and institutions.
  • Jainism has its own distinct spiritual lineage, recognizing a succession of 24 Tirthankaras, with Mahavira as the 24th in the current cycle. Its philosophy emphasizes non-violence, disciplined conduct, asceticism, and liberation of the soul from karmic bondage.
  • Sikhism emerged in Punjab in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries through Guru Nanak and the succession of Sikh Gurus. It emphasizes devotion to the One, honest work, service to others, and human equality, and developed its own religious identity while engaging with the wider spiritual environment of the subcontinent.

These histories also show the importance of examining religious institutions critically. Religious authorities have sometimes used their positions to reinforce hierarchies, justify exclusion, or protect political power. At other times, religious communities have challenged injustice, preserved knowledge, supported the vulnerable, and inspired social reform.

The same complexity applies beyond India. Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Zoroastrianism arose in different historical settings and hold distinct beliefs about God, revelation, morality, and the purpose of human life. They share ethical concerns such as honesty, charity, responsibility, and compassion, but they don't teach precisely the same theology or philosophy.

It would be an oversimplification to say every religion began with a purely benevolent founder and was later corrupted by power-seekers. Religious traditions have always been shaped by a mix of spiritual conviction, philosophical disagreement, political authority, cultural change, and ordinary human needs. Religious history is neither a single story nor a simple split between truth and corruption. It is a long history of ideas being created, transmitted, challenged, and reinterpreted, and reading these traditions together lets us appreciate their individual contributions while examining their claims with curiosity and care.

The Global Pattern: Prophets and Institutional Control

Across Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, we see traditions that convey the same core moral imperatives: love thy neighbor, speak the truth, give to the poor, humble the ego, and honor the Creator. Yet history shows a sobering, recurring pattern:

  • The Pure Founder: A prophet, sage, or awakened teacher (Krishna, Buddha, Zarathustra, Moses, Jesus, or Muhammad) walks the earth as a compassionate visionary trying to uplift humanity and free people from hypocrisy and greed.
  • The Hijacking After Their Departure: After the teacher passes, ambitious people with power-hungry intent step into the vacuum. Recognizing the emotional power of the following, emperors, politicians, and clerical elites build rigid institutions around the prophet's name.
  • Religion as an Instrument of Control: The teachings of love and inner liberation are rewritten or weaponized to enforce obedience, collect taxes, divide humanity into "us versus them," and control the masses through fear.

From a scholar's bird's-eye view, the light at the core of human spirituality has always been one and the same. The divisions exist only in the human institutions built around it.

If you belong to another religion, I would genuinely like to hear a creation story, a figure, or a teaching from your tradition that deserves more attention, especially a version specific to your language, region, family, or community. It would also help to hear when a comparison in this post seems inaccurate or misses an important distinction. People who are not religious can contribute historical or philosophical perspectives too.

The idea is for all of us to learn a little about traditions beyond our own, describing them fairly and respecting the people who live by them. We can disagree about religious truth and still be curious about each other's stories.
